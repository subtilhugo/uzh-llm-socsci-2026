\begin{frame}{The Author's Own Reading: Two Worlds}

\textbf{World 1.} This is real research. But it has nothing to do with my expertise. I cannot evaluate it. Some other human expert has to.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\textbf{World 2.} This is not real research. I just produced a negative externality by polluting the information environment.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{His conclusion.} In a world of machine-speed papers, we have a problem either way.
\alert{\textbf{Generation speed $\gg$ verification speed.}} This was never the case historically.
Anybody, with no expertise, can now produce a paper that looks legitimate at first glance.

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\small Open questions he lists: matching in the academic labour market, credit attribution. ``I don't have the answers.''}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\centering \textbf{Neither do I. But we can push this a little further, and it changes what we do in this room.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\chg{Pushing Further (1):} The Bottleneck Moved}

\textbf{His two worlds have one consequence in common.}
Either way, the scarce resource is no longer production. It is \alert{qualified verification}. The bottleneck moved from \emph{doing} to \emph{judging}.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.58\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Why did this never happen before?}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Verification was always slow. But generation was slow too.
    \item The cost of writing a paper was a \textbf{filter}: you did not write one you did not believe in.
    \item It was also a \textbf{signal} of competence (Spence 1973): costly, hence informative.
    \item Peer review was calibrated for a world where submitting cost a year.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.38\textwidth}
\centering

\begin{frame}{\chg{Pushing Further (2):} Verification at Machine Speed}

\textbf{This is not hypothetical. People have already looked at the APE papers from the outside.}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cunningham (2026)} went through 651 APE papers: systematic p-hacking patterns\chg{.}
    \item \textbf{Ning (2026)} compared 912 APE papers with 41 \chg{\emph{AER} and \emph{AEJ} papers: the human papers win 83\% of head-to-head comparisons, and most of the gap is on the \emph{idea} (originality, relevance, choice of design), not on execution.}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{The detail that matters for us.} To verify 651 papers, Cunningham used a large language model. The APE tournament itself is judged by LLM referees.

\vspace{0.25cm}

\centering {\Large \alert{\textbf{Who verifies the verifier?}}}

\vspace{0.25cm}

\raggedright
{\small Verification at machine speed is possible. It is also the same machine. The chain has to end with a human who understands what was done.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{\chg{Pushing Further (3):} The Dependence Trap}

\textbf{The capacity to verify is produced by doing.}
If nobody does the thing any more, then within one cohort nobody can check the thing. And when nobody can check, everybody depends on whoever still can.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{The Covid version.}
Countries that had stopped making basic goods (masks, paracetamol) found they could not make them when it mattered. Not because it is hard. Because they had handed the know-how to someone else.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{The academic version.}
The senior PI who delegates everything to research assistants and stops checking. When it breaks, it is \emph{their} name on the paper. \alert{\textbf{Responsibility does not delegate.}}

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\small An agent is a research assistant who never gets tired, never says ``I am not sure'' unless you ask, and bears no responsibility at all.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Trade-off, and Why It Is Yours to Make}

\textbf{Short term, individual.}
Delegating everything is rational. You will produce more, faster. The productivity imperative is real, and your career is short.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\textbf{Long term, collective.}
Expertise is a common-pool resource. Every person who stops understanding depletes it a little. Nobody feels it until it is gone.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{And the feeling of productivity is not productivity.}
In a randomised trial (Becker et al.\ 2025), experienced open-source developers were \alert{\textbf{19\% slower}} with AI tools on their own code, while believing they had been about 20\% faster. A narrow setting, but keep the gap between the feeling and the measurement.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\centering
\textbf{I am not going to pretend this trade-off is balanced.\\ It is your choice. Here is mine.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{My Position, and Three Rules for This Workshop}

\begin{alertblock}{Where I stand}
I am not here to teach you the technique. I am here to show you how to delegate it.
But I think we have a moral obligation to understand what we delegate.
Otherwise we will not know how to do it, and when nobody knows, we all depend on someone else.
\end{alertblock}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{You delegate execution, never understanding.} You must be able to explain every file in your project.
    \vspace{0.15cm}
    \item \textbf{Read before you accept.} Every output is a draft. Every session has a mandatory manual check.
    \vspace{0.15cm}
    \item \textbf{Stop when you no longer know what it is doing.} Supervising agents is work. Fatigue is the signal to stop, not to accept faster.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\centering
\textbf{This is exactly why we do not start with a chat.\\ We start with a folder.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Chat vs Agent}

\textbf{A chat}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You paste; it answers; you copy. It sees what you paste, nothing else.
    \item It forgets everything when you close the tab.
    \item It cannot open your PDF, run your script, or look at your data.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{An agent}
\begin{itemize}
    \item It works \textbf{inside a folder} on your machine.
    \item The loop: \textbf{read} files $\rightarrow$ \textbf{run} commands $\rightarrow$ \textbf{write} files $\rightarrow$ report.
    \item It asks permission before each action that changes something.
    \item Claude Code, Gemini CLI, Codex: same idea, different vendors.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\centering
\textbf{The permission prompt is the only thing that separates\\ an assistant from an unsupervised intern.} Keep it on. Read it.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why a Folder, Not a Chat}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Context.} The agent reads \texttt{CLAUDE.md} at the start of every session. You explain your project once, in a file, not in every conversation.
    \vspace{0.2cm}
    \item \textbf{Memory.} \texttt{PROJECT\_HISTORY.md} says where the project stands. A fresh session can answer ``where are we?'' from the file alone. Nothing lives in the chat.
    \vspace{0.2cm}
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility.} Git, versioned prompts, scripts that regenerate outputs from raw data. In a year you will know what was done, from what, and by whom.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{And one more: the folder is yours.}
Change tool tomorrow, the folder stays. The same \texttt{CLAUDE.md} works in the terminal, in Cursor or VS Code, in the desktop app. A chat history belongs to a vendor; a folder belongs to you.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of the Project Template}

\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.57\textwidth}
{\scriptsize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\texttt{my-project/} & \\
\texttt{\ \ README.md} & \textcolor{color1bg}{\textbf{what the project is}} \\
\texttt{\ \ CLAUDE.md} & \textcolor{color1bg}{\textbf{how the agent behaves}} \\
\texttt{\ \ PROJECT\_HISTORY.md} & \textcolor{color1bg}{\textbf{where we stand}} \\
\texttt{\ \ literature/} & PDFs (read-only) \\
\texttt{\ \ notes/literature/} & one note per paper \\
\texttt{\ \ notes/} & memos, critiques \\
\texttt{\ \ data/raw/} & never modified \\
\texttt{\ \ data/processed/} & \chg{rebuilt by scripts} \\
\texttt{\ \ code/} & numbered scripts \\
\texttt{\ \ outputs/} & figures, tables \\
\texttt{\ \ prompts/} & the prompts, versioned \\
\texttt{\ \ paper/} & drafts \\
\texttt{\ \ .gitignore} & \texttt{.env}, data, PDFs \\
\end{tabular}
}

\column{0.39\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Three files carry everything.}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\texttt{README.md}: question, design, data, people. Stable facts.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\texttt{CLAUDE.md}: what the agent may and may not do, your conventions. Read at every start.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\texttt{PROJECT\_HISTORY.md}: dated log. Done, decided, \emph{verified by a human}, open.
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\small The rest is just folders. Copy the template, \texttt{git init}, and the agent knows the rules before you say a word.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Guardrails}

\textbf{Written in \texttt{CLAUDE.md}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{data/raw/} is read-only. Always.
    \item Never invent a reference, a number, a result. Not in the folder = ``not found''.
    \item Never delete. Never push. Nothing leaves the folder unless you asked.
    \item Every claim about a paper cites file and page.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{Written in your habits}
\begin{itemize}
    \item API keys live in \texttt{.env}; \texttt{.env} is in \texttt{.gitignore}.
    \item Confidential data: a sample, anonymised, or never near an API.
    \item One agent at a time. Read the diff.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\centering
A rule in a file is a request, not a guarantee. \alert{\textbf{You will see one fail today.}}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What You Leave With at Noon}

A git folder for \textbf{your} project, containing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{README.md} with your question, in your words
    \item \texttt{CLAUDE.md} with your conventions
    \item 3 to 5 literature notes, one \textbf{verified by you} against the PDF
    \item a one-page research memo
    \item a hostile critique of that memo, and your response
    \item an entry in \texttt{PROJECT\_HISTORY.md} and a first commit
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{And one thing you will have seen:} the agent making a mistake in a literature note. A wrong number, a wrong page, a finding that is not in the paper.
Finding it is step 5 of the worksheet. \alert{\textbf{It is the only step you cannot skip.}}

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\small No project yet, or no PDFs today? Use the example project. You lose nothing.}

\end{frame}
