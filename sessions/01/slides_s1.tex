\begin{frame}{The Author's Own Reading: Two Worlds}

\textbf{World 1.} This is real research. But it has nothing to do with my expertise. I cannot evaluate it. Some other human expert has to.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\textbf{World 2.} This is not real research. I just produced a negative externality by polluting the information environment.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{His conclusion.} In a world of machine-speed papers, we have a problem either way.
\alert{Generation speed $\gg$ verification speed.} This was never the case historically.
Anybody, with no expertise, can now produce a paper that looks legitimate.

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\small And two questions he leaves open: if anyone can produce a paper, how do we tell who is good, and who gets the credit? ``I don't have the answers.''}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\centering Neither do I. But we can push this a little further\ldots

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pushing Further (1): The Bottleneck Moved}

His two worlds have one consequence in common.
Either way, the scarce resource is no longer production. It is \alert{qualified verification}. The bottleneck moved from \emph{doing} to \emph{judging}.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{columns}[c]
\column{0.56\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Why did this never happen before?}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Verification was always slow. But generation was slow too.
    \item The cost of writing a paper was a filter: you did not write one you did not believe in.
    \item It was also a signal of competence (Spence 1973): costly, hence informative.
    \item Peer review was calibrated for a world where submitting cost a year.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.40\textwidth}
\centering

\begin{frame}{Pushing Further (2): Verification at Machine Speed}

The APE papers have already been checked from the outside.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{itemize}

    \vspace{0.15cm}
    \item \textbf{Ning (2026)}, 912 papers against 41 from the \emph{AER} and \emph{AEJ}: the human papers win 83\% of comparisons. The gap is in the \emph{idea}, not in the execution.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4cm}

Both checks were run with LLMs. So is the APE tournament.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\centering {\Large \alert{Who verifies the verifier?}}

\vspace{0.4cm}

The chain has to end with a human who understands.

\begin{frame}{Pushing Further (3): What You Cannot Delegate}

\textbf{The skill to check.}
It is built by doing. Stop doing, and within one cohort nobody can check any more. Everyone then depends on whoever still can.

\vspace{0.1cm}
{\small During Covid, countries that had stopped making masks and paracetamol could not make them when it mattered.}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{The accountability.}
You can delegate a task, not the responsibility for it. If the agent is wrong, it loses nothing: no reputation, no retraction, no referee to answer. Your name is on the paper.

\vspace{0.1cm}
{\small The senior PI who lets the RAs do everything and stops checking: when it breaks, it is still the PI's paper.}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\centering
\alert{Delegate the work. Keep the skill to check it,\\ and the responsibility for it.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Trade-off, and Why It Is Yours to Make}

\textbf{Short term, individual: publish or perish.}
Delegating everything is rational. ``Nobody's tombstone reads `Wrote his own \LaTeX\ code'\,'' (Spina 2026).

\vspace{0.25cm}

\textbf{Long term, collective.}
Expertise is a common-pool resource. Every person who stops understanding depletes it a little.

\vspace{0.25cm}

\textbf{But is publish or perish from another age?}
When anyone can produce a paper, a paper stops being a signal. If what stays scarce is the idea, and the ability to vouch for the result, the game will be redefined. Not tomorrow: committees still count papers. And you are hit first: the tasks you learn on are the ones agents take over.

\vspace{0.2cm}

{\small And the feeling of productivity is not productivity: in a randomised trial, experienced developers were \alert{19\% slower} with AI tools, while believing they were 20\% faster (Becker et al.\ 2025).}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\centering
I am not going to pretend this trade-off is balanced.\\ It is your choice. Here is mine.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{My Position, and Three Rules for This Workshop}

\begin{alertblock}{Where I stand}
I am not here to teach you the technique. I am here to show you how to delegate it.
But you remain accountable for what you delegate, so you have to understand it.
Otherwise, when nobody knows how to do it any more, we all depend on someone else.
\end{alertblock}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{You delegate execution, never understanding.} You must be able to explain every file in your project.
    \vspace{0.1cm}
    \item \textbf{Read before you accept.} Every output is a draft. Every session has a mandatory manual check.
    \vspace{0.1cm}
    \item \textbf{Stop when you no longer know what it is doing.} Fatigue is the signal to stop, not to accept faster.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\small Why rule 3: the frontier is jagged. On a task just outside what the model could do, consultants with GPT-4 were 19\% \emph{less} likely to get it right than without it (Dell'Acqua et al.\ 2023). \alert{Knowing where the frontier lies is a skill, and you learn it by doing.}}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Costs We Will Not Study Here}

\small
\textbf{Environmental.} Data centres: 415 TWh in 2024, 1.5\% of world electricity, more than double by 2030 (IEA 2025). One prompt is small, about nine seconds of TV (Google 2025), but an agent chains many.

\vspace{0.25cm}

\textbf{Human, collective.} Behind the models, low-paid workers. In Kenya, people labelled violent and sexual content for ChatGPT's safety filter for \$1.32 to \$2 an hour (Perrigo 2023).

\vspace{0.1cm}
{\footnotesize Both are the subject of my critical course, \emph{Artificial Intelligence and/for the Social Sciences}: \href{https://subtilhugo.github.io}{\textcolor{titleblue}{subtilhugo.github.io}}.}

\vspace{0.25cm}

\textbf{Personal.} Supervising is tiring: constant vigilance, reviewing work you did not write. 77\% of employees who use AI say it added to their workload (Upwork 2024). Being dispossessed of your own work erodes the sense of accomplishment, a core dimension of burnout (Maslach \& Leiter 2016). \alert{Take care of yourselves.}

\vspace{0.35cm}

\centering
This is exactly why we do not start with a chat.\\
We start with a folder: a project you own, read and understand.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Chat vs Agent}

\textbf{A chat}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You paste; it answers; you copy. It sees what you paste, nothing else.
    \item It forgets everything when you close the tab.
    \item It cannot open your PDF, run your script, or look at your data.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{An agent}
\begin{itemize}
    \item It works inside a folder on your machine.
    \item The loop: read files $\rightarrow$ run commands $\rightarrow$ write files $\rightarrow$ report.
    \item It asks permission before each action that changes something.
    \item Claude Code, Gemini CLI, Codex: same idea, different vendors.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\centering
Same model as in the chat. What changes is the loop, the tools, and the permission prompt.\\
\alert{That prompt is what separates an assistant from an unsupervised intern. Keep it on.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why a Folder, Not a Chat}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Context.} The agent reads \texttt{CLAUDE.md} at the start of every session. You explain your project once, in a file, not in every conversation. A long chat degrades as it fills up; a file does not.
    \vspace{0.2cm}
    \item \textbf{Memory.} \texttt{PROJECT\_HISTORY.md} says where the project stands. A fresh session can answer ``where are we?'' from the file alone. Nothing lives in the chat.
    \vspace{0.2cm}
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility.} Git, versioned prompts, scripts that regenerate outputs from raw data. In a year you will know what was done, from what, and by whom.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\textbf{And one more: the folder is yours.}
Change tool tomorrow, the folder stays. The same \texttt{CLAUDE.md} works in the terminal, in Cursor or VS Code, in the desktop app. A chat history belongs to a vendor; a folder belongs to you.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of the Project Template}

\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.57\textwidth}
{\scriptsize

\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\texttt{my-project/} & \\
\texttt{\ \ README.md} & \textcolor{titleblue}{what the project is} \\
\texttt{\ \ CLAUDE.md} & \textcolor{titleblue}{how the agent behaves} \\
\texttt{\ \ PROJECT\_HISTORY.md} & \textcolor{titleblue}{where we stand} \\
\texttt{\ \ literature/} & PDFs (read-only) \\
\texttt{\ \ notes/literature/} & one note per paper \\
\texttt{\ \ notes/} & memos, critiques \\

\texttt{\ \ data/processed/} & rebuilt by scripts \\
\texttt{\ \ code/} & numbered scripts \\
\texttt{\ \ outputs/} & figures, tables \\

\texttt{\ \ paper/} & drafts \\
\texttt{\ \ .gitignore} & \texttt{.env}, data, PDFs \\
\end{tabular}
}

\column{0.39\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Three files carry everything.}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\texttt{README.md}: question, design, data, people. Stable facts.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\texttt{CLAUDE.md}: what the agent may and may not do, your conventions. Read at every start.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\texttt{PROJECT\_HISTORY.md}: dated log. Done, decided, \emph{verified by a human}, open.
\end{columns}

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\small The rest is just folders. Copy the template, \texttt{git init}, and the agent knows the rules before you say a word.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Guardrails}

\small
\textbf{Written in \texttt{CLAUDE.md}}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{data/raw/} is read-only. Always.
    \item Never invent a reference, a number, a result. Not in the folder = ``not found''.
    \item Never delete. Never push. Every claim about a paper cites file and page.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\textbf{Written in your habits}
\begin{itemize}
    \item What the agent reads is sent to the vendor's servers; what it does not open stays on your machine. Confidential data: a sample, or a script that uses the file without the agent opening it.
    \item API keys live in \texttt{.env}; \texttt{.env} is in \texttt{.gitignore}.
    \item Stay in ``ask'' mode: it proposes, you approve. Never switch off permissions.
    \item One agent at a time. Read the diff.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\centering
\normalsize
A rule in a file is a request, not a guarantee. \alert{You will see one fail today.}

\end{frame}

\end{itemize}

Finding it is step 5 of the worksheet. \alert{It is the only step you cannot skip.}
